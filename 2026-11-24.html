Top stories

1. Jensen Huang is back as Nvidia CEO, eleven days after resigning

Nvidia's board reinstated founder Jensen Huang as CEO, president, and board member on Nov 23, citing input from senior U.S. officials on national security — Latent Space reports the President dialed into the board meeting. The news sent markets soaring. Colette Kress remains CFO, the independent directors' investigation into the affair and espionage allegations continues, and Huang's Senate hearing is still due in about a week; the board also formed a committee for national security and export control.

Why it matters: The leadership crisis that defined Nvidia's last two weeks is over in one sense — the founder is back with explicit White House backing — but the governance questions are not: the investigation and the Senate hearing are still pending, and the episode raises the uncomfortable question of whether one CEO has become too central to America's AI strategy to be removed.

Sources: AI Daily Brief, Latent Space

2. OpenAI and Cisco launch Shirudo, an open-source shield for unpatched Linux systems

Shirudo (Japanese for "shield") is an open-source runtime that uses eBPF to protect running Linux systems while patches are still being prepared — instead of blocking a vulnerable operation outright (which can take a service offline), teams test a temporary "shield" that blocks only the exploit behavior and deploy it without restarting. In a demo, a Codex-generated shield blocked a Java4Shell exploit attempt against a running Elasticsearch service. Shields are cryptographically signed, verified before loading, and logged via TPM.

Why it matters: Finding a vulnerability is now often the fast part; patching every affected system takes days or weeks, and that gap is where exploits land. Shirudo turns the patch window from dead time into defended time — and because it's open source, a shield written once can be reused by everyone running the same software.

Sources: OpenAI News

3. Google Search gets personal: Wallet and Drive join Personal Intelligence in AI Mode

Google is extending Personal Intelligence in Search's AI Mode beyond Gmail, Calendar, and Photos: users can now opt in to connect Google Wallet and, in the coming weeks, Drive and the Gemini app, so Search answers draw on spending, documents, and chats. Examples Google gives: mapping spending against financial goals, optimizing credit-card rewards from linked cards, and building plans from scattered docs. Wallet data is read-only — AI Mode can't move or invest money — and connections can be toggled off at any time.

Why it matters: This is the other half of the personal-AI bet: not a smarter model, but a model with access to your life. The utility is obvious — Search that knows your budget and your files — and so is the trust question, which is why Google is leaning hard on opt-in controls and a visible "Personalization" indicator showing what context was used.

Sources: Google AI Blog

4. Microsoft joins OpenAI's perplexity pact: MAI-2 Base scores 0.819 on OpenAI's eval set

Following yesterday's Pachocki essay proposing shared pretraining limits, Microsoft became the first lab to cosign — running its MAI-2 Base model on OpenAI's evaluation set and reporting 0.819 log-perplexity, in GPT-5.6 Sol base-model territory. Latent Space's read of the essay adds texture: an unreleased OpenAI internal model already sits below GPT-6.2 Astra, and Pachocki expects another capability jump in the ~0.68–0.70 window, possibly as soon as 2027, with a "steep acceleration in 2026."

Why it matters: A shared metric only works if more than one lab reports it. Microsoft joining makes the proposal real rather than rhetorical — but the test is whether Anthropic and the other frontier labs follow. The numbers also set a public clock: if the curve keeps falling at this rate, the next capability jump lands within months, not years.

Sources: Latent Space, OpenAI News

5. Meta's Muse launches in the UK

Muse, Meta's personal AI agent, is now available to adults in the UK — its first expansion beyond the US. It connects to email, calendar, and retailer apps to handle jobs like chasing refunds or renewing car insurance, with a separate security agent checking actions against granted permissions, and approval required before anything sensitive like sending an email or making a purchase. Meta says Muse conversations and stored information are not shared with its ad systems.

Why it matters: Agentic assistants are now a geographic rollout story, not just a capability one — and the UK launch is the first test of whether Meta's control-first pitch (approvals, visible audit trail, data kept out of ads) travels to a market with stricter privacy expectations.

Sources: Meta Newsroom, Meta Newsroom
