1. Google launches Gemini Decision 1, a purpose-built model for fast, calibrated decisions

Google has entered the decision-model market with Gemini Decision 1 and a new Decisions API. Instead of generating text token by token, the model scores candidate options in a single forward pass, returning probabilities and confidence scores for up to 1,000 yes/no, multiple-choice, or rubric-scored questions against one multimodal input (text, image, audio, video, or PDF, up to 256K tokens). End-to-end latency is 150–250 ms, priced at $0.10 per 1M input tokens with zero output tokens billed — about 5x cheaper than Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite on the same benchmark suite, per Google. On Google's tests, Decision 1 beats TypeSafe's Jev 1.14 on 5 of 7 shared benchmarks, including 92.2% vs 90.0% on JevBench, though it trails Drex Max on cost-per-task and on some long-document tasks. LangChain shipped day-zero support, and Google's own Stitch tool demoed a rapid-design experiment built on the API.

Why it matters: This is the "System One" moment going mainstream: a frontier lab shipping a small, non-generative model as a first-class product, not a helper. Decision models are the missing piece for agents that need to choose — tool selection, routing, policy checks, grading — cheaply and reliably instead of burning a reasoning model on every branch. Google beating Anthropic to a production decision model, weeks after Jev kicked off the trend in September, turns a niche into a product category with real competition.

Sources: Google AI Blog · Latent Space

2. Anthropic's IPO: surging revenue, huge compute bills, and a valuation nobody can pin down

NLW's AI Daily Brief takes on the Anthropic IPO, now the defining question of the AI trade: revenue is surging and the company has a new productivity suite taking aim at Microsoft, but it also carries massive compute commitments. The episode breaks down the competing bull and bear cases and argues the valuation headlines are misleading — Anthropic is a different kind of business from the public comparables Wall Street keeps reaching for. In the headlines: Google's Gemini 4 Carbon release (covered yesterday), Nvidia's deepening China scandal, and OpenAI's revenue surge.

Why it matters: Anthropic's listing will be the first public market test of a frontier lab's economics — and the first chance for ordinary investors to buy a pure-play AI model company. Whatever price it lands at will set the reference point for the whole AI trade, from the other labs to the infrastructure names. The uncertainty itself is the story: the market has no agreed model for valuing a company whose biggest asset is a model and whose biggest liability is the compute to run it.

Sources: AI Daily Brief

3. a16z: welcome to the B2A economy — agents are becoming customers

a16z argues the primary users of the internet are no longer human: agents now make up the majority of web traffic, write more than half of new articles published online, and generate most of the code pushed to production. As agents move from answering questions to completing tasks, they will increasingly decide which products to use, which services to subscribe to, and where to spend money on our behalf. The evidence is already measurable: Walmart says AI-driven traffic to its stores grew eightfold year over year in Q1 2026, with orders from AI-powered searches up nearly thirteenfold; Human Security saw agentic browser traffic grow 8x between August and October, with Muse driving the largest share (60%). AI-referred shoppers engage more and convert better than non-AI traffic, with a lower bounce rate.

Why it matters: Every prior platform shift paired a new capability with a new distribution mechanism, and AI has so far overperformed on the first and underperformed on the second. If agents become a genuine distribution channel — a "business-to-agent" economy — the companies that treat agents as first-class customers (machine-readable storefronts, agent-native checkout) get a new wave of growth, and the ones that kept building walls to keep bots out find themselves competing for bots' business instead.

Sources: a16z News

4. The Jev revolution: "System One" models go from demo to production

At the AI Engineer Code Summit, Jev creator Diogo Almeida called on developers to "automate everything" with system-one models — small, non-generative models that return structured choices, scores, or probabilities instead of generated text. His argument: LLMs are "Einstein 95% of the time, but Mr. Bean 5% of the time — and if you can't tell which is which, how are you going to automate anything." Glean showed Jev in production alongside LLMs and other classifiers, valuing it because the decision space can change without re-fine-tuning. Cloudflare showed a hybrid design where fast decision models handle tool selection and log extraction, with a large reasoning model called in only for diagnosis — cutting six LLM calls from a build-failure flow.

Why it matters: This is the practical counterpart to Google's Decision 1 launch (story 1): the engineering community is converging on a two-tier architecture — cheap, fast, calibrated decision models inside application code, big reasoning models reserved for the genuinely hard parts. If the pattern holds, it changes what "AI engineering" looks like: decision-model calls sprinkled through code rather than ever-longer prompts and ever-more-convoluted chains.

Sources: Latent Space · Latent Space

5. OpenAI commits $2M to research on AI and children's safety

OpenAI has signed a children-first declaration and committed $2 million to research on AI and children's safety, supporting shared tools to measure risks and opportunities for young people. (OpenAI's article page returned a 403 to our fetcher today, so this summary is from the feed description.)

Why it matters: Child safety is the issue most likely to bring regulation to AI products, and it is also where the labs' own commitments are most closely watched. A $2M research fund is small money for OpenAI, but shared measurement tools — the thing the commitment actually funds — are the practical bottleneck: nobody can set policy, or build safer products, without agreed ways to measure what harms and helps young users.
