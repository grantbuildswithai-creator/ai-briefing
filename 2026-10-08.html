1. a16z: Why Texas is making data centers wait

Texas — the promised land for AI data centers — is slamming the brakes. a16z's analysis digs into why: at the end of 2024, the state's grid operator was staring at interconnection requests totaling more than five times record peak demand, about 90% of it data centers. Developers routinely spam speculative projects across multiple sites, many with no customer and no GPU experience, so planners can't tell which requests are real. The response: new rules forcing data centers to pay for their own grid upgrades, and a de facto pause on new permits — plus growing community pushback over noise, water, emissions, and power bills.

Why it matters: The AI buildout is colliding with physical infrastructure. For a decade, hyperscalers could just sign up as large loads on grids with spare capacity; that era is over. Where (and whether) data centers get power is becoming as strategic as the chips inside them.

Sources: a16z

2. Claude Haiku 5.5, benchmarked: better than GPT-6 Luna at the same price

Following yesterday's launch news, Latent Space's AINews has the detailed third-party numbers. Haiku 5.5 scores 43 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index — up 26 points from the last Haiku, ahead of GPT-6 Luna (38), GLM-5.3 Flash (42), and Gemini 3.8 Flash (41). It's priced to match Luna directly, costs ~75% less to run than Haiku 4.5, and is the first Haiku with Anthropic's effort settings and adaptive thinking. Anthropic also cut Sonnet 5.5 prices and subscription plan prices the same day, and baked computer-use toolsets into the Claude SDKs.

Why it matters: The small-model contest has become an open price war, and Anthropic is positioning Haiku explicitly as the subagent workhorse — "Opus does the heavy thinking, Haiku does the high-volume work." Agentic workloads live or die on cost per task, so this is where the next round of competition happens.

Sources: Latent Space

3. Kubernetes' creators want to run your AI agents like cattle, not pets

Craig McLuckie and Joe Beda — two of the people who created Kubernetes — are building Stacklok, a cloud-native harness for coding agents. The pitch: coding agents started as terminal tools and desktop apps (Claude Code began as a CLI), but enterprises can't manage fleets of agents that live on individual laptops, where the code, context, and IP actually sit. Their product Mecatl applies Kubernetes-style control loops to agent sessions: keeping them running, recovering from failures, isolating tool execution, preserving context — in the cloud.

Why it matters: It's the enterprise-ization of the agent stack in real time. The interesting pattern: every layer of the AI stack is re-learning the last decade of cloud infrastructure lessons, and the people who built that infrastructure are now selling the sequel.

Sources: Latent Space
