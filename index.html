Top stories

1. Meta takes its AI age-estimation global to put more teens in protected accounts

Meta is expanding its AI age-estimation technology worldwide, aiming to place millions more teens into Teen Account protections on Instagram even when they signed up with an adult birthday. The system looks for signals that an account belongs to a teen and applies built-in limits on who can contact them, what content they see, and time spent — protections that previously depended on self-reported age. The rollout is extending to Facebook and, later this year, Threads, and builds on Meta's recent agreement with 52 bipartisan US attorneys general to strengthen how it identifies teens and children under 13. In Australia, Meta is also starting to use Apple's age-range signal from the App Store as an additional check, and it is pressing for app-store age verification to become mandatory rather than optional.

Why it matters: Age assurance is one of the most concrete, large-scale deployments of AI inference in consumer tech — and one of the most contested, since it trades on estimating a user's age from behavioral signals. Meta's global push, plus its call for a single verified device-level signal shared across apps, shows the industry converging on a model where platforms infer age rather than trust what users type, with regulators watching closely.

Sources: Meta Newsroom

2. Stanford rewrites its engineering course for the age of coding agents

Stanford's CS146S, "The Modern Software Developer," has been redesigned around a simple fact: every student surveyed already codes with an agent, and last year's homework can now be one-shot in a single prompt. In the new run, students contribute to real open-source projects, where a maintainer reviews their patch — forcing them to learn the judgment agents skip: whether a change belongs, why the code is the way it is, and what else it might break. Instructor Mihail Eric, who also leads AI at Monaco, reports his team kept Devin for code review but built its own agent workspaces, and built "Hal," a system that investigates outages and opens PRs for engineers while giving salespeople plain-language explanations of how the product works. He predicts open-source repos will become agent-run software factories — and argues the durable skill is deciding which change belongs.

Why it matters: The course is a live experiment in what engineering education — and junior engineering work — becomes when implementation is cheap. The answer emerging from Stanford and Monaco alike: the scarce skills shift from writing code to evaluating it, understanding a codebase's history, and owning the decision to merge. Teams with high AI adoption in the episode's data merged 98% more PRs, while review time grew 91% longer — a tradeoff every engineering org is now navigating.

Sources: Latent Space
