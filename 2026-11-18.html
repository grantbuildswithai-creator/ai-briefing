AI Briefing — Wed, Nov 18, 2026

Top stories

1. OpenAI ships GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol — cheaper, nearly as capable, and divisive

OpenAI released GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol on Nov 17. Astra scores 62 on the Intelligence Index, second only to Anthropic's Fable 5.2 at 65, but at a fraction of the cost: Sol ties September's Astra at about 15% of the cost per task, and OpenAI claims Astra at its highest effort beats GPT-6 Astra by 5.2 points at under a third of the cost. Astra is priced at $8/$40 per million input/output tokens ($0.40 cached input); Sol at $2/$10. Both have a 1M-token context window and 128K output limit, and are rolling out to ChatGPT Work, Codex, and the API.

Why it matters: The launch is an efficiency play, not a capability leap — OpenAI is now competing on intelligence per dollar, and by that measure it claims the lead. But early tester reaction was unusually split: some called Astra "essentially unusable" after weeks of testing, while others praised specific wins (a walkable Singapore built from 100+ Codex threads; a leading SVG peacock in visual coding). Also notable: OpenAI rated Astra "High" for AI Self-Improvement, a first for one of its externally released models.

Sources: OpenAI (article fetch failed — connection closed; summary from the feed description and Latent Space), Latent Space

2. Nvidia posts $115.8B quarter under interim CEO; board says Huang won't return

In its first earnings report since the CEO transition, Nvidia posted $115.8B in quarterly revenue, up 103% year over year, and said Vera Rubin NVL72 has shipped. The board said it does not intend to reinstate Jensen Huang. The print lands amid a push from some quarters to bring Huang back and new U.S. chip export controls under consideration in Congress.

Why it matters: The numbers are strong, but the governance question is now the story: the board has formally closed the door on Huang's return while the interim-CEO and investigation questions from the Q3 call remain unresolved. Export controls add a policy overhang on top of it.

Sources: Latent Space, AI Daily Brief (headlines)

3. Business AI use is surging — but spending barely is

In October, businesses tracked by Ramp used 36% more AI tokens while spending just 2% more. Data from Ramp, Databricks, and Arcana show companies switching models and expanding AI use beyond coding, while autonomous agent adoption still lags.

Why it matters: Falling prices are doing the work: enterprises are consuming far more AI without paying far more, which is good for adoption and bad for anyone whose business model assumed token prices would hold. The lag in autonomous agents is the gap to watch — usage is broadening, but hands-off deployment is not yet following.

Sources: AI Daily Brief (episode page show notes; no full transcript)

4. Microsoft and Salesforce launch Copilotforce

Unveiled at Microsoft Ignite, Copilotforce brings Salesforce customer context, business processes, and actions into Microsoft Copilot, Teams, and Fabric. It includes Salesforce in Copilot (public preview, 38+ prebuilt sales skills), two-way Zero Copy integration between Salesforce Data 360 and Microsoft Fabric, and authenticated Salesforce MCP access so sellers can act inside their existing Salesforce permissions. Accenture is design and implementation partner and pilot customer.

Why it matters: The two biggest enterprise software ecosystems are betting that the next productivity gain comes from joining the CRM to the meeting and the document, not from a new standalone agent. The Zero Copy angle also matters: no duplicate datasets to govern.

Sources: Microsoft

5. Cloudflare calls itself a "neolab" and starts building its own models

Cloudflare says it is now building its own models, starting with System One models — fast, small models that make decisions in milliseconds instead of routing everything to a full LLM. Its first two are Clef (a take on TypeSafe AI's Jev, built in two weeks) and Clairic, an extraction model that pulls typed values from unstructured input in one forward pass. The company frames model-building as a natural step from infrastructure to compute to storage, and as a way to derisk its AI operations.

Why it matters: The "neolab" label is marketing, but the direction is real: infrastructure companies that sit on the request path can build small, specialized models where LLMs are overkill — and Cloudflare's latency-first DNA is well suited to it. Expect more infra players to follow.

Sources: Latent Space
