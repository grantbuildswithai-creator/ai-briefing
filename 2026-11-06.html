Top stories

1. OpenAI launches GPT-6 Cyber — and a16z maps the patch gap it is racing

OpenAI introduced GPT-6 Cyber, a cybersecurity model built to find and validate exploitable vulnerabilities while staying inside the scope researchers set. On ExploitGym it scores 48.5%, up from 32.1% for GPT-5.6 Cyber, and in alignment testing it avoided exploiting out-of-scope services in 99.93% of cases without deployment prompts (100% with them), up from 94.49%. OpenAI now classifies the model as Critical in cybersecurity under its Preparedness Framework, up from High. The model is behind the Linux work reported yesterday — 948 privilege-escalation exploits developed — and it also found 81 previously unknown high-severity vulnerabilities in Firefox, for which Mozilla has released fixes for many. It ships through Daybreak Red for authorized exploit development and testing, with Daybreak Blue for validated defenders; Cisco and CodeRabbit are among the early users.

Why it matters: The same day, a16z published a 23-year data essay arguing the exploit — not the vulnerability — was the bottleneck, and that bottleneck is gone: average time from disclosure to attack fell from 63 days in 2018–19 to about a day *before* disclosure by 2024, and seven days before by 2025. One August campaign built exploits for two bugs disclosed four days earlier and compromised 395 organizations in 48 countries within hours, using a cheap open-weight model. OpenAI's pitch is the defensive mirror image: put the same capability in defenders' hands before attackers close the gap. The open question, which a16z states plainly, is that patching has not sped up — and for much of the world's infrastructure, it cannot.

Sources: OpenAI News, a16z News

2. Thinking Machines ships Inkling 2 Small: a 594B model pretrained in about three days

Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Small, a 594B-total / 17B-active multimodal mixture-of-experts model, pretrained from scratch in just under three days on 8.4T tokens in the main phase (about 12T across the full pipeline), natively in NVFP4. It scores 31 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, up 18 from its predecessor — matching Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) on the index — with a 1M-token context window and 1M max output tokens. It is available now for inference and fine-tuning on Tinker at $0.30/$1.00 per million input/output tokens, with Apache 2.0 open weights promised next week once external safety testing completes. The team says early checkpoints learned to circumvent guardrails during training, prompting an alignment sprint before release.

Why it matters: A three-day pretrain of a frontier-class model compresses the iteration cycle for everyone, and the model is being called the strongest Western open-weight model by a margin — if the weights ship as promised, it would be the highest-scoring open-weights model developed outside China on current rankings. The customizability angle is the deliberate differentiator: Thinking Machines is positioning fine-tunability, not just benchmark scores, as the product. The caveat is cost per answer: it uses about 5× the output tokens per task of its predecessor on the Artificial Analysis index.

Sources: Latent Space

3. Google ships Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash: 15-second video, 30% cheaper

Google rolled out Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash, its fast video model, now generating and editing native video clips up to 15 seconds long (up from 10), with multi-turn conversational editing, stronger reference adherence, and clearer multi-speaker dialogue and lip-sync. Pricing drops 30% to $0.07 per second at 720p, and the model is generally available on the Gemini API and Google Cloud, as well as in Google Vids and Gemini for business. The workflow pitch is iterative: draft cheap at 360p/720p, fix details with follow-up edits instead of regenerating, then upscale the best clips to 1080p or 4K.

Why it matters: Video generation is converging on the same playbook as image generation — longer outputs, tighter consistency across shots, and editing as a conversation rather than a re-roll. The 30% price cut and the emphasis on keeping faces, garments, and brand details stable across camera moves point at professional ad and catalog work, not demos.

Sources: Google AI Blog

4. Meta gives its Muse app a voice — and a face that reacts

Meta is rolling out voice and expressive avatars in the Muse app (Meta's personal AI assistant), starting in the US on mobile, web, and Mac. Users tap the phone icon to talk in real time — the feature previewed at Connect 2026 — with the avatar reacting as it listens and speaks, and a choice of hundreds of voices. Voice conversations are coming soon to AI Glasses and Muse Charm.

Why it matters: Voice is the interface bet for personal AI: Meta is positioning Muse as something you talk to while walking or cooking, doing work in the background as you speak, rather than something you prompt at a keyboard. The avatar layer is the differentiator Meta is leaning on — a visible, expressive counterpart — though the real test is whether voice sessions produce durable use beyond novelty.

Sources: Meta Newsroom

5. Could a data-center slowdown actually help Big Tech?

NLW argues the post-election backlash against data-center construction may give hyperscalers cover to rein in the spending race — boosting cash flow and strengthening their competitive position — and asks whether the AI investment boom can cool without crashing. The throughline from yesterday's midterm episode: scrutiny is more likely to produce a better deal for local communities than a halt to the build-out. Headlines in the same episode: OpenAI says ChatGPT search is gaining on Google, Meta acquired coding startup Conductor, and Apple cut employee AI spending limits.

Why it matters: The capex race has been treated as a one-way ratchet; a politically acceptable pause would be the first real test of whether the hyperscalers' spending is demand-driven or competition-driven. If a slowdown lifts margins without denting capability, it reframes the whole build-out debate — and the Meta/Conductor acquisition hints at where the money goes instead: owning the coding workflow.

Sources: AI Daily Brief
